The investment thesis

A market is a network of decision-makers operating under constraints. A change in prices can force position changes; those trades can change liquidity and prices again. Understanding the feedback is a potentially valuable complement to knowing portfolio exposures.

Our initial product would make that process inspectable for a portfolio manager: specify an event, compare a mechanism-based scenario with a simple baseline, challenge the assumptions and retain a trace of the analysis.

The bet: a rigorously evaluated, narrowly scoped simulation workflow can earn repeated use first, then become infrastructure for understanding financial interactions.

Why this deserves attention now

The Bank of England’s 2024 system-wide exercise found that collective actions can amplify shocks and exposed mismatches in firms’ expectations of one another. This is a concrete reason to study interactions, not proof of demand for our product. Primary source ↗

Our timing thesis is that a small team can now combine explicit simulation with AI-assisted document processing and calibration tooling to test a useful workflow at a bounded cost. The opportunity must still be earned through better decisions, usable outputs and buyer evidence.

Different markets. The same unanswered question.

Across very different episodes, portfolios that appeared separate came under pressure at the same time. The public record can establish the losses and, in some cases, plausible transmission mechanisms. It cannot reveal every position, constraint or trade. That gap is the problem we want to make testable.

What these cases establish — and what they do not.

They show a recurring, costly class of interaction risk. They do not prove that one mechanism caused every drawdown, that MicroWorld could have forecast them, or that buyers will pay for our proposed workflow. Our job is to turn competing explanations into explicit mechanisms, then test them against data and simple baselines.

Traditional risk tools describe exposures, crowding and stress outcomes. The next question is dynamic: when another participant is forced to respond, how does that action change the market state—and when does your portfolio become exposed?

The first 90 days: an answerable test.

After initial capital, staffing and data access are in place, test a bounded ten-trading-day downside-risk target separately from workflow usefulness. Historical replay can diagnose mechanisms; it cannot, by itself, establish forecast skill.

Window Deliverable Decision gate
Days 1–30 Data-rights map, point-in-time snapshots, frozen evaluation specification and credible baselines. No evaluation or commercial use beyond the permitted data rights. No changing the target after seeing results.
Days 31–60 Calibrated bounded mechanism, historical simulation, EWMA and quantile-regression comparisons; mechanism ablations. Equal information and tuning budgets. Remove components that do not add dependable information.
Days 61–90 Held-out results, uncertainty intervals, failure analysis and an external methodological review. Continue, narrow or stop the predictive claim based on the predeclared evidence.

Proposed scientific release criterion

One candidate threshold is at least 5% lower held-out quantile loss than the strongest preregistered simple baseline, with a paired 95% uncertainty interval supporting improvement. This is a proposed threshold to freeze before evaluation, not an achieved result or an industry standard.

Use ten-day cumulative returns, purge overlapping labels across splits, hold out time and sectors, and estimate uncertainty with joint date blocks. Simulation seeds are not independent real-world observations. Test whether removing the interaction mechanism removes the gain.

Run customer discovery in parallel

Target five or more task-specific interviews and qualify two possible shadow pilots. A pilot only counts once there is a decision owner, permitted data, an agreed task and an evaluation. Interviews and interest do not count as revenue or commitments.

The world-model horizon

Our long-term ambition is an interactive model of financial institutions, heterogeneous participant populations and their changing constraints. A user would propose an intervention and explore a distribution of possible responses, with assumptions and uncertainty visible.

1. Observable state

Combine licensed prices, liquidity indicators, permitted holdings, disclosures and institutional constraints into versioned, time-aligned states. Missing positions and unobserved incentives remain explicit uncertainty; delayed disclosures are not real-time holdings.

2. Structured transitions

Begin with auditable rules. Learn effective parameters only where the data can identify them. Agent-based models organize heterogeneous participants; mean-field methods are a candidate for tractable population interactions. They must earn their complexity against simpler alternatives.

3. Adaptation and cross-market interaction

Study regime-dependent behavior, funding and liquidity links, and how populations change their policies. Learn reusable representations and transition approximations only after demonstrating external validity. A fast surrogate is useful only if it preserves the behavior that matters.

4. Intervention and evaluation

Build toward experiments on alternative constraints, policy settings and portfolio decisions. A credible model must distinguish an observed correlation from an identified mechanism, report uncertainty and decline unsupported extrapolation.

5. Distribution beyond one team

If the foundation works, embed validated capabilities in investment platforms and eventually explore finer-grained participant behavior. Individual-level modeling raises harder observability, consent, privacy and validation problems; it is a research direction, not an available capability.

Five-year success would mean: a trusted environment for exploring financial interactions, with evidence that transfers across more than one event, regime or customer. It would not mean a machine that knows every position or predicts every crisis.

The hard questions

Why invest before the real-market claim is validated?

The early investment case is a bounded, falsifiable research program paired with a concrete commercial workflow and a much larger expansion path. The current demo proves that an inspectable experience can be built. Investment judgment still depends on founder execution, defensible data access and the quality of the next experiments.

What if many parameter sets explain the same price path?

That is a central identification risk. Price-only calibration can confound participant scale, trading strength and market impact. We need external flow, positioning or depth evidence where available, uncertainty analysis and held-out tests. A convincing replay alone is insufficient.

Why not let an LLM simulate every investor?

Language models can help extract constraints and communicate outputs. We start with explicit transition rules so that a result can be traced, rerun and challenged. An LLM narrative is not a calibrated probability distribution. More complex agents must justify their inclusion empirically.

Is the path in the demo a forecast or proof of causality?

No. It is a synthetic consequence of chosen rules. Comparing feedback on and off is a controlled model ablation. It is not a real-world causal estimate, investment recommendation or validated ten-day forecast.

Why does this team have a chance to win?

The founders combine mathematics and AI, engineering, and finance. The existing research direction gives a starting point. Those backgrounds are not a moat: execution must be demonstrated through reproducible results, disciplined customer learning and the ability to recruit or collaborate where expertise is missing.

What do you need from the first investor?

A partner willing to evaluate an early technical thesis, agree the first evidence gate and help finance its execution. Relevant introductions to buyers, independent reviewers and later lead investors can be useful alongside capital. No such support is represented here as committed.
